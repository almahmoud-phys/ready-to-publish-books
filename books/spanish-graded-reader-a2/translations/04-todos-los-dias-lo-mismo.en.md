# Story 4 — The Same Thing Every Day

In Puerto Lento every day is the same.

At four thirty, Miguel Ortiz gets up and walks to the port. Miguel fixes the boat engines. He is
forty-one years old and his hands are always dirty.

At five, Rosa opens the bakery.

At five thirty, the boats go out to sea.

At six, Beto opens the boat shed. Always at six. Beto is not a perfect person,
but he opens the shed at six every day of the year.

At seven in the evening, Ana opens the kiosk.

At one in the morning, Ana closes it.

Ana knows this order perfectly. After three months, she knows it without thinking.

That is why, today, Ana wants to do something different.

Ana wants to give the letter back. But to give the letter back, she needs to know who leaves it in the
kiosk. And that person does not come by day.

So Ana does not sleep. She goes to bed at two and gets up at four.

At four in the morning the town is dark and cold. Ana walks to the pier. There is nobody. She
sits down near the kiosk and waits.

At four thirty Miguel passes by. He sees her.

"Ana? What are you doing here?"

"Nothing. I can't sleep."

Miguel says nothing more and walks on to the port.

At five, there is a light in the bakery.

At five thirty, the boats go out.

At six, Ana looks at the boat shed.

The shed is closed.

Ana waits. Five minutes. Ten minutes. Beto does not come.

This never happens. Beto is late for family things, late for meals, late for
everything. But he is not late for the shed. The shed is the one serious thing in his life.

Ana looks at the clock again. Six fifteen.

At six twenty, Ana walks toward the shed. And then she sees that the door is not closed. The
door is open a little.

Inside there is a light.

Ana goes in.

It is not Beto.

Miguel Ortiz is sitting on the floor with an open box. The box is full of old
papers. Miguel has a paper in his hand.

"Miguel."

Miguel does not get up. He does not seem nervous. Only tired.

"Good morning, Ana."

"What is that?"

Miguel looks at the box.

"Papers."

"Whose papers?"

Miguel waits a moment. Then he hands the paper to Ana.

It is a list of names. It is old. Ana reads the names and finds one:

**Tomás Ferrer**

"Miguel, what is this list?"

"It's a list of people who work here. From many years ago."

"And why is it here, in a box, on the floor?"

Miguel takes the paper and puts it back in the box.

"Because nobody wants to decide," he says.

"Decide what?"

"His things are here. His name is on the list. His table is in the bakery." Miguel closes
the box. "To take his things away, someone has to say that he isn't coming back. And nobody wants to say
that. So everything stays the same. The same thing every day."

Ana understands.

"Miguel, do you know who leaves letters in my kiosk?"

Miguel stands up. He picks up the box. He walks toward the door.

"There's nothing, Ana," he says.

And he leaves.

Ana is alone in the shed. She thinks about Beto, on the pier, saying the same words.

There's nothing.

Now Ana understands that it is not a lie. Nobody says "there's nothing" because there is nothing.

They say it because they do not want to decide.
